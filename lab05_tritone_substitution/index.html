Tritone Substitution

Replace a dominant by another dominant whose root lies a tritone away. The root, bass motion and colour change; the two guide-tone pitch classes that define the dominant tritone remain locally invariant.

What changes, and what remains invariant?

1. The local invariant

Compare G7 and D♭7. Their roots are a tritone apart, but their 3rd and 7th are the same two sounding pitches, with their harmonic roles exchanged.

Original dominant
G7
G · rootB · 3rdD · 5thF · 7th
⇄
Tritone substitute
D♭7
D♭ · rootF · 3rdA♭ · 5thC♭ · 7th
Invariant guide-tone pair: B/F in G7 becomes C♭/F in D♭7. On an equal-tempered piano, C♭ and B are the same sounding pitch; the spelling changes because the harmonic function changes.

2. Put the substitution in a real harmonic phrase

A tritone substitution is easier to hear when the ear has first established the key and the dominant occupies a clear cadential slot. Here the familiar diatonic phrase I–vi–ii–V–I provides that context. Only the dominant slot changes.

Original context · I–vi–ii–V–I
Cmaj7 → Am7 → Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7
Bass: C → A → D → G → C
Upper voices are kept in a compact register so that they form continuous melodic strands across the phrase.
Substituted context · I–vi–ii–subV–I
Cmaj7 → Am7 → Dm7 → D♭7 → Cmaj7
Bass: C → A → D → D♭ → C chromatic cadence
The tonic, vi and ii establish C major before D♭7 replaces G7 in the same functional position.

3. Follow genuine voice lines through the cadence

The four parts now keep their identity from Dm7 through the substituted dominant into Cmaj7. No voice is reassigned merely to make a chord stack fit.

Listen for the difference: the substitute produces three especially clear semitone motions at the cadence—D→D♭→C in the bass, A→A♭→G above it, and F→F→E in the guide voice. The common B/C♭ pitch is retained into Cmaj7 as B rather than being forced upward to C.

4. Build the substitution step by step

1
Start with the dominant.
G7 = G–B–D–F.
2
Keep the guide-tone tritone.
Preserve B/F as sounding pitch classes.
3
Reinterpret their functions.
F becomes the 3rd and B is respelled C♭, the 7th.
4
Change the root by a tritone.
G → D♭, and supply A♭ as the new 5th.
5
Resolve to the same target.
Both dominants can point toward C, but the substitute gives D–D♭–C chromatic bass motion in context.

5. Enharmonic spelling is structural information

MIDI stores pitch, not harmonic spelling. The same key/pitch (MIDI 59) is named differently according to harmonic role.

This is why the lab displays C♭ even though the audio sample is the same exact piano key as B. The spelling tells us what the pitch is doing harmonically.

What changed?

  • the dominant root: G → D♭
  • the fifth: D → A♭
  • the harmonic spelling and functional role of B/C♭
  • the bass path: D–G–C becomes D–D♭–C
  • the colour and surface voice leading

What remained invariant?

  • the target: Cmaj7
  • the dominant function at the local level
  • the two guide-tone pitch classes F and B/C♭
  • the tritone carried by those guide tones
  • directed resolution toward the tonic

Why this matters for the blues

Tritone substitution now becomes one of the explicit transformation rules available for the next synthesis lab. In the blues, students will be able to hear what happens when this local operation is introduced into a larger harmonic form.
